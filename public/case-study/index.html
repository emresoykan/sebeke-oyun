Calibrating a game's power price model to market data and linking it to project economics

In Wattlas, players build renewable power plants on a world map. This note explains how the game's hourly electricity price model was calibrated to 2025 market data, and what the same model implies for LCOE, NPV and IRR at real-world scale. The game interface is in Turkish.

Summary

  • Problem: The game's Turkish market pushed midday prices down to 4–9 $/MWh. Because unsold output was curtailed, the solar plant's capacity factor stayed at 8%. Financial metrics computed from this model were meaningless.
  • Approach: Targets for the average price and the capture rate were derived from EPİAŞ 2025 data (Türkiye) and ENTSO-E 2025 data (Germany, Spain). Model parameters were then tuned against these targets using 365-day in-game simulations.
  • Result: In Türkiye the game now produces an average price of 65.8–66.8 $/MWh (actual: 66.4). The solar capture rate is 73–74% (actual: ~75%) and wind 95–99% (actual: 99%). In Europe the average price is 78–91 $/MWh (target: ~89) and the solar capture rate 50–57% (target: ~50%).
  • Finding: At a 10% cost of capital, a solar plant built at the same cost is at break-even in Türkiye. In Europe the sign of its NPV depends on the simulated weather year. The difference comes from capture rate and curtailment, not from the solar resource.

1. Why it mattered

For each plant, the game's project economics module takes generation and the average realised price from the simulation. Capex and opex are real-world assumptions. If the simulated price is far from reality, LCOE, NPV and IRR are wrong too: garbage in, garbage out. For the module to mean anything, the price model had to be tested against market data first.

The game's hourly price combines a base daily curve, seasonal demand, evening heating demand, a midday price reduction from solar and a general price reduction from wind. Calibration sets the size of each component.

3. Method

Capture rate: the average price a technology earns in the hours it generates, divided by the flat (baseload) average price. Solar plants all generate at the same hours, so they depress midday prices; the more solar capacity, the lower the ratio. This effect is called cannibalisation.

capture price = Σ (price × generation) ÷ Σ generation capture rate = capture price ÷ average price
  1. Derive targets: compute both metrics from actual data, separately for Türkiye and Europe. Europe is a single market in the game, so the simple average of Germany and Spain was used as the target.
  2. Measure in the game: run the game headless in a browser, build one solar plant and one wind farm, advance 365 game days and measure the same metrics. The game's plants curtail at a zero price, so the "market" solar capture rate counts curtailed output as sold at zero. That is the figure comparable to actual data.
  3. Tune and repeat: find the cause of each deviation, change the relevant parameter and measure again.

Why it deviated and what changed

  • The market's solar saturation rose by 0.012 per day and hit its cap in about three months. After that, the midday price fell to nearly zero almost every day. The daily increase was cut to 0.002 and the cap was limited to 1.2 times the starting value.
  • The depth of solar's midday price reduction was cut from 57 to 30 $/MWh and wind's effect from 10 to 8. The base price level was raised by 8%.
  • In Europe the price level multiplier was raised from 1.15 to 1.9. The solar effect was set to 2.5 times Türkiye's and the wind effect to 3 times. This reflects the data: in both countries solar and wind depress prices more strongly than in Türkiye (lower capture rates).

5. Project economics: the same solar plant in two markets

Using the calibrated model, a 5 MW solar plant and a 5 MW wind farm were run for 365 days in each market. Generation and realised prices come from the simulation; costs come from real-world assumptions.

Interpretation

  • Solar in Türkiye is at break-even. The average realised price (≈$49/MWh) is only slightly above LCOE (≈$47/MWh). With an IRR of 10.6–10.7%, the project does not create value at any higher cost of capital. A 2-point change in WACC flips the sign of NPV: for an unsubsidised, merchant-only solar plant, the cost of financing is decisive.
  • Higher prices in Europe, lower returns. Europe's average price is higher than Türkiye's. But prices often fall to zero in solar hours, and the game's plant curtails 18–28% of its output. As the capacity factor falls, the same capex is spread over fewer MWh, and LCOE rises to $57–65/MWh. Returns are driven by capture rate and curtailment, not by irradiation.
  • Weather-year risk. For European solar, NPV ranged from −$0.62m to +$0.43m across three simulated years with the same settings. This shows why price and curtailment risk in a merchant solar project is worth reducing through a long-term power purchase agreement (PPA) or a battery.
  • Read the wind results as optimistic. The selected sites are good wind sites in the game, not fleet averages. IRENA's global average capex may also be lower than project costs in a given country. Both effects push IRR up.

6. Limitations

  • Small samples: the Türkiye solar ratio rests on 7 days, Europe on 12. Daily values are very volatile; in Germany the solar ratio ranged from −57% to 137% on single days. The targets show direction and magnitude, not exact annual values.
  • Unlicensed solar missing: solar in EPİAŞ real-time generation covers licensed plants only. The profile's shape is indicative.
  • No negative prices: the game's price floor is 0 and plants curtail at a zero price. Real markets have negative prices, and subsidised plants keep generating. That is why curtailment in the game's European market is high.
  • Single European market: the difference between Germany and Spain is lost in the average.
  • Game pacing: the game's own economy (game money) is deliberately compressed. The financial metrics in this note are computed at real-world scale, not in game money.

7. Next steps

  • Re-measure Türkiye's solar capture rate with a full year of hourly solar data.
  • Add negative prices and support schemes to the model.
  • Split Europe into separate country markets.
  • Use pilot player feedback to measure which parts are seen as unrealistic.
