Case study • October 2026

Calibrating a game's power price and generation model to market data and linking it to project economics

In Wattlas, players build renewable power plants on a world map. This note explains how the game's hourly electricity price and wind generation models were calibrated to 2025 data, and what the same model implies for LCOE, NPV and IRR at real-world scale. The game interface is in Turkish.

Update (30 Oct 2026): The results on this page were produced with the 18 October 2026 version of the model, over 365 game days (about three calendar years in the game). The game has since been recalibrated: the price level in Türkiye and Europe now matches 2025 data over the first game year (120 days), and the price effect of wind in Europe is regional. The figures on this page have not been regenerated; the current calibration and measurements are in the repository's docs/kalibrasyon.md.

Summary

  • Problem: The game's Turkish market pushed midday prices down to 4–9 $/MWh. Because unsold output was curtailed, the solar plant's capacity factor stayed at 8%. Wind generation was also off, in different directions by country: too low in Türkiye, more than twice the actual level in Germany. Financial metrics computed from this model were meaningless.
  • Approach: Targets for the average price and the capture rate were derived from EPİAŞ 2025 data (Türkiye) and ENTSO-E 2025 data (Germany, Spain), and targets for the wind fleet capacity factor from TEİAŞ, EPDK and ENTSO-E. Model parameters were then tuned against these targets using 365-day in-game simulations.
  • Result: In Türkiye the game now produces an average price of 65.4–65.8 $/MWh (actual: 66.4). The solar capture rate is 73% (actual: ~75%) and wind 98–99% (actual: 99%). The wind fleet capacity factor is 32.6% in Türkiye (actual: 32.8%) and 20.2% in Germany (actual: 20.2%). In Europe the average price is 89–91 $/MWh (target: ~89) and the solar capture rate 53–59% (target: ~50%).
  • Finding: At a 10% cost of capital, a solar plant built at the same cost is at break-even in both markets; Europe's higher price is eroded by curtailment and a low capture rate. For wind, resource quality decides: the Çanakkale plant earns an IRR of 20.5–22%, the northern German one 11–12%.

1. Why it mattered

For each plant, the game's project economics module takes generation and the average realised price from the simulation. Capex and opex are real-world assumptions. If the simulated price is far from reality, LCOE, NPV and IRR are wrong too: garbage in, garbage out. For the module to mean anything, the price model had to be tested against market data first.

The game's hourly price combines a base daily curve, seasonal demand, evening heating demand, a midday price reduction from solar and a general price reduction from wind. Calibration sets the size of each component. A wind farm's output is computed from the site's hourly wind speed through a turbine power curve; the site's mean speed comes from NASA POWER data.

3. Method

Capture rate: the average price a technology earns in the hours it generates, divided by the flat (baseload) average price. Solar plants all generate at the same hours, so they depress midday prices; the more solar capacity, the lower the ratio. This effect is called cannibalisation.

capture price = Σ (price × generation) ÷ Σ generation capture rate = capture price ÷ average price
  1. Derive targets: compute both metrics from actual data, separately for Türkiye and Europe. Europe is a single market in the game, so the simple average of Germany and Spain was used as the target.
  2. Measure in the game: run the game headless in a browser, build one solar plant and one wind farm, advance 365 game days and measure the same metrics. The game's plants curtail at a zero price, so the "market" solar capture rate counts curtailed output as sold at zero. That is the figure comparable to actual data.
  3. Tune and repeat: find the cause of each deviation, change the relevant parameter and measure again.

Why it deviated and what changed

  • The market's solar saturation rose by 0.012 per day and hit its cap in about three months. After that, the midday price fell to nearly zero almost every day. The daily increase was cut to 0.002 and the cap was limited to 1.2 times the starting value.
  • The depth of solar's midday price reduction was cut from 57 to 30 $/MWh and wind's effect from 10 to 8. The base price level was raised by 8%.
  • In Europe the price level multiplier was raised from 1.15 to 1.9. The solar effect was set to 2.5 times Türkiye's and the wind effect to 3 times. This reflects the data: in both countries solar and wind depress prices more strongly than in Türkiye (lower capture rates).

Wind generation

Capacity factor (CF): a plant's annual output divided by what it would produce running at full power all year. Fleet CF = total generation ÷ (installed capacity × hours).

  • Power curve: a modern turbine like the game's standard one (150 m rotor) reaches rated power at 11 m/s, not 12.
  • Site speed factor: the ~50 km wind data is biased differently by country. In Türkiye wind farms sit on ridges windier than the grid-cell average, so the factor is 1.02; in Germany it is 0.73 and in Spain 0.925. Each factor was chosen so that the game's fleet CF matches actual output. In Türkiye the fleet was weighted by the EPDK provincial distribution.
  • Seasonality: monthly speed multipliers from the 2025 monthly CF of Türkiye, Germany and Spain. Türkiye's windiest month is August (summer northerlies); Germany's is winter.
  • Indicator: the wind dots in the panel are now an expected-CF class. ●●●○○ means 27–34% and includes the Turkish fleet average.

Financing structure: debt, tax and PPA

The same plants were modelled with debt and corporate tax under two sales structures. Under spot, all output is sold to the market. Under a PPA, a fixed price 10% below the expected realised price applies for the first 10 years. The bank sizes the debt so that the minimum DSCR over the tenor meets a target: 1.40 for spot sales, 1.20 under a PPA. The loan has a 12-year tenor with level instalments, and gearing is capped at 75%. In the stress columns the debt is sized on the base case, then the spot price falls by 20%.

Interpretation

  • Solar is at break-even in both markets. In Türkiye the average realised price (≈$48/MWh) is level with LCOE ($47–51/MWh); IRR is 9.2–10.2%. A 2-point change in WACC decides the sign of NPV: for an unsubsidised, merchant-only solar plant, the cost of financing is decisive.
  • In Europe the higher price is eroded by curtailment. Europe's average price is ~37% higher than Türkiye's. But prices often fall to zero in solar hours, and the game's plant curtails 17–21% of its output. With a lower capacity factor the same capex is spread over fewer MWh and LCOE rises to $57–60/MWh. Returns end up close to Türkiye's.
  • Weather-year risk. Across simulated years with the same settings, solar NPV moved to both sides of zero. This shows why price and curtailment risk in a merchant solar project is worth reducing through a long-term power purchase agreement (PPA) or a battery.
  • For wind, resource quality decides. The same turbine runs at a 45–48% capacity factor in Çanakkale and 23–24% in northern Germany. Although the German price is ~28% higher, IRR stays at 11–12%, against 20.5–22% in Çanakkale. Çanakkale is one of Türkiye's windiest sites; at the fleet average (33%) and the same price, IRR falls to ~13.5%.
  • A PPA is insurance. In the base case the PPA's equity return is below spot, because the price is discounted by 10%. In return the bank lends more; for Turkish solar, gearing rises from 42–47% to 55–58%. The real difference shows in the bad case: when the spot price falls by 20%, the Çanakkale wind farm's equity IRR drops to 20–21% under spot but only to 26–27% under the PPA.
  • Leverage only helps when the project return exceeds the cost of debt. Turkish solar's after-tax project IRR (8.4–9.6%) is almost the same as the interest rate (8.6%). So debt barely lifts the equity return (8.7–11.0%). For the Çanakkale wind farm the project IRR is 16%, so the same debt lifts the equity return to 33–34%.
  • If the tenor outlasts the PPA, risk piles up at the end of the loan. The loan runs 12 years, the PPA 10. The last two years are exposed to the market. Under price stress, the minimum DSCR of PPA-backed Turkish solar can fall below 1 (0.96–1.03). This is why lenders want the tenor to match the contract term.
  • The cost assumption may be optimistic. IRENA's global average capex may be lower than project costs in a given country; this pushes all IRRs up.

6. Limitations

  • Small samples: the Türkiye solar ratio rests on 7 days, Europe on 12. Daily values are very volatile; in Germany the solar ratio ranged from −57% to 137% on single days. The targets show direction and magnitude, not exact annual values.
  • Unlicensed solar missing: solar in EPİAŞ real-time generation covers licensed plants only. The profile's shape is indicative.
  • No negative prices: the game's price floor is 0 and plants curtail at a zero price. Real markets have negative prices, and subsidised plants keep generating. That is why curtailment in the game's European market is high.
  • Single European market: the difference between Germany and Spain is lost in the average. That is why Europe's wind capture rate stays above target (91–94% against ~83%): in the game German wind now produces in winter, when prices are high, and raising the wind effect in the price model pushed the average price far too low.
  • Wind targets rest on a single year: the 2025 wind year may differ from the long-term average. Fleet averages include older turbines, while the game's plant is new. The regional weights for Germany and Spain are approximate.
  • Game pacing: the game's own economy (game money) is deliberately compressed. The financial metrics in this note are computed at real-world scale, not in game money.

7. Next steps

  • Re-measure Türkiye's solar capture rate with a full year of hourly solar data.
  • Add negative prices and support schemes to the model.
  • Split Europe into separate country markets.
  • Use pilot player feedback to measure which parts are seen as unrealistic.
