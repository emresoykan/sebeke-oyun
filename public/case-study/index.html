Case study • October 2026

Calibrating a game's power price and generation model to market data and linking it to project economics

In Wattlas, players build renewable power plants on a world map. This note explains how the game's hourly electricity price and wind generation models were calibrated to 2025 data, and what the same model implies for LCOE, NPV and IRR at real-world scale. The game interface is in Turkish.

Calibration and project economics now rest on the first game year (120 game days, about one calendar year); the first version used the average of 365 game days (about three calendar years). The European targets now use full-year ENTSO-E 2025 data, and the Turkish solar target was widened from 7 to 12 sample days.

Summary

  • Problem: The game's Turkish market pushed midday prices down to 4–9 $/MWh. Because unsold output was curtailed, the solar plant's capacity factor stayed at 8%. Wind generation was also off, in different directions by country: too low in Türkiye, more than twice the actual level in Germany. Financial metrics computed from this model were meaningless.
  • Approach: Targets for the average price and the capture rate were derived from EPİAŞ 2025 data (Türkiye) and ENTSO-E 2025 data (Germany, Spain), and targets for the wind fleet capacity factor from TEİAŞ, EPDK and ENTSO-E. Model parameters were then tuned against these targets using simulations of the first game year (120 game days).
  • Result: In the first game year the average price in Türkiye is 66.6 $/MWh (actual: 66.4). The solar capture rate is 84% (actual: 83.4%) and wind 97% (actual: 98.6%). The wind fleet capacity factor is 32.6% in Türkiye (actual: 32.8%) and 20.2% in Germany (actual: 20.2%). In the European market, calibrated to Germany, the average price is $102.1/MWh (actual: ~$101), the solar capture rate 48–51% (51.6%), wind 89% (86.6%) and negative-price hours 6.5% (6.6%). In Iberia, covering Spain and Portugal, the average price is $74.7/MWh (~$74), solar 54% (55.2%), wind 97% (96.5%) and negative-price hours 6.6% (6.3%).
  • Finding: If first-year prices hold, a merchant solar plant is profitable at a 10% cost of capital in Türkiye (IRR 10.9–12.7%) but not in Spain (5.4–7.8%). The Spanish plant curtails 23–35% of its output in zero- and negative-price hours and sells the rest at 74–76% of the average price, against 78–86% for the Konya plant; capex is also 13% higher. For wind, resource quality decides: the Çanakkale plant earns an IRR of 18.8–25.3%, the northern German one 11.5–15.8%.

1. Why it mattered

For each plant, the game's project economics module takes generation and the average realised price from the simulation. Capex and opex are real-world assumptions. If the simulated price is far from reality, LCOE, NPV and IRR are wrong too: garbage in, garbage out. For the module to mean anything, the price model had to be tested against market data first.

The game's hourly price combines a base daily curve, seasonal demand, evening heating demand, a midday price reduction from solar and a general price reduction from wind. Calibration sets the size of each component. A wind farm's output is computed from the site's hourly wind speed through a turbine power curve; the site's mean speed comes from NASA POWER data.

3. Method

Capture rate: the average price a technology earns in the hours it generates, divided by the flat (baseload) average price. Solar plants all generate at the same hours, so they depress midday prices; the more solar capacity, the lower the ratio. This effect is called cannibalisation.

capture price = Σ (price × generation) ÷ Σ generation capture rate = capture price ÷ average price
  1. Derive targets: compute both metrics from actual data, separately for Türkiye and Europe. Europe was at first a single market in the game, and the average of Germany and Spain was the target. Since 11 Nov 2026 Iberia (Spain and Portugal) is a separate market: its target is Spain, Europe's is Germany.
  2. Measure in the game: run the game headless in a browser and advance the first game year: 120 game days, with one game day covering about three calendar days. Rivals invest as in a normal game; event cards are off. Ratios are measured at market (fleet) level: the price is weighted by the market's solar generation profile and the wind fleet's hourly output, including output in hours when the price is zero. That is the figure comparable to actual data.
  3. Tune and repeat: find the cause of each deviation, change the relevant parameter and measure again.

Why it deviated and what changed

  • The market's solar saturation rose by 0.012 per day and hit its cap in about three months. After that, the midday price fell to nearly zero almost every day. The daily increase was cut to 0.002 and the cap was limited to 1.2 times the starting value.
  • The depth of solar's midday price reduction was cut from 57 to 30 $/MWh and wind's effect from 10 to 8. The base price level was raised by 8%.
  • In Europe the price level multiplier was raised from 1.15 to 1.62. The solar effect was set to 3.7 times Türkiye's and the wind effect to 1.7 times. This reflects the data: in both countries solar and wind depress prices more strongly than in Türkiye (lower capture rates). After the split into two markets: Europe 1.69 / 4.0 / 1.45, Iberia 1.19 / 2.6 / 0.97. In Spain the price level is lower and wind's price effect weaker.
  • In Europe, wind's price effect is no longer computed from the wind speed at a single representative point but from the hourly output of the wind fleet, weighted by region (Germany's regions for Europe, Spain's for Iberia). Wind blows across a region at once; when the fleet produces a lot, the price falls.
  • In the game, prices fall over the years because rivals build plants every year. The target was therefore defined on the first game year, and Türkiye's price level multiplier was set to 0.92: the first-year average fell from ~72 to ~66 $/MWh.

Wind generation

Capacity factor (CF): a plant's annual output divided by what it would produce running at full power all year. Fleet CF = total generation ÷ (installed capacity × hours).

  • Power curve: a modern turbine like the game's standard one (150 m rotor) reaches rated power at 11 m/s, not 12.
  • Site speed factor: the ~50 km wind data is biased differently by country. In Türkiye wind farms sit on ridges windier than the grid-cell average, so the factor is 1.02; in Germany it is 0.73 and in Spain 0.925. Each factor was chosen so that the game's fleet CF matches actual output. In Türkiye the fleet was weighted by the EPDK provincial distribution.
  • Seasonality: monthly speed multipliers from the 2025 monthly CF of Türkiye, Germany and Spain. Türkiye's windiest month is August (summer northerlies); Germany's is winter.
  • Indicator: the wind dots in the panel are now an expected-CF class. ●●●○○ means 27–34% and includes the Turkish fleet average.

Financing structure: debt, tax and PPA

The same plants were modelled with debt and corporate tax under two sales structures. Under spot, all output is sold to the market. Under a PPA, a fixed price 10% below the expected realised price applies for the first 10 years. The bank sizes the debt so that the minimum DSCR over the tenor meets a target: 1.40 for spot sales, 1.20 under a PPA. The loan has a 12-year tenor with level instalments, and gearing is capped at 75%. In the stress columns the debt is sized on the base case, then the spot price is cut by 20%.

Interpretation

  • Solar earns more than a 10% WACC in Türkiye. In Türkiye the average realised price ($52–57/MWh) is above LCOE ($45–52/MWh); IRR is 10.9–12.7%. At a 12% WACC, NPV falls on both sides of zero (−$0.25m to +$0.17m): for an unsubsidised, merchant-only solar plant, the cost of financing remains decisive.
  • The result depends on first-year prices holding. In the game, prices fall over the years as rivals invest: in Türkiye the average price is $67/MWh in the first year, $61 in the second and $54 in the third (−19%; normal year, 4 runs, 10 Nov 2026). For solar the fall is faster: as rivals add solar, its capture rate drops from 0.83 to 0.78 and 0.74 (~5 points a year), and solar's market-wide realised price falls from ~$56 to ~$40/MWh (−28%). The 20% price stress in the financing table understates this risk for solar. In reality, while the solar fleet grew ~28% a year (TEİAŞ: 20.2 GW in 2024, 25.9 GW in 2025), the solar capture rate fell from 0.91 to 0.83 (~8 points). Later years are the game's own behaviour, not a forecast. The lower returns in the first version of this page (Turkish solar IRR 9.2–10.2%) came from the average price of 365 game days, i.e. three years including that decline.
  • In Spain, solar is worth far less than the market average. Iberia's average price in these runs is ~13% higher than Türkiye's ($74.5 against $66.1/MWh). But prices fall deeply in solar hours: the midday (11–14) average is $28–37/MWh, and on spring middays the price is often below zero. The plant does not generate in zero- and negative-price hours: it curtails 23–35% of its output and sells the rest at $55–57/MWh ($52–57 in Konya). Its capacity factor falls to 15–18%, and capex is 13% higher ($779 against $691/kW). LCOE ($65–78/MWh) is above the realised price: IRR is 5.4–7.8%, and NPV is negative even at an 8% WACC (−$0.80m to −$0.05m). Spain's actual 2025 solar capture price is ~$41/MWh ($74 × 55.2%); in the game the rate counting curtailed output is 50–59%.
  • A model error was reversing the project decision. Until 10 Nov 2026 Europe was a single price zone. Solar's price effect was computed from the cloud cover at a representative point in central Germany (50°N 10°E), while the plant generated under its own site's weather. When Spain was sunny and Germany cloudy, the plant sold at a high price: a capture rate of 63–69% counting curtailed output, a realised price of $65–71/MWh and an IRR of 10.7–13.3%. With Spain and Portugal as a separate market (Iberia), the rate fell to 51–56% (actual 55.2%) and IRR to 4.8–6.7% (5.4–7.8% once negative prices were added, as the plant no longer generates in those hours): the same plant went from "profitable" to "loss-making". A correct market average (the single European market was on target at 55%) is not enough to validate a single plant's result.
  • Weather-year risk. Each run is a different weather year. The northern German wind farm's capacity factor ranged from 20% to 26% across runs, and its NPV from +$0.60m to +$2.47m, more than a four-fold difference between the best and worst year. This is why projects are judged on a long-term generation estimate rather than a single year; for a merchant project, a PPA or a battery reduces this risk.
  • For wind, resource quality decides. The same turbine runs at a 41–56% capacity factor in Çanakkale and 20–26% in northern Germany. Although the German realised price is ~40% higher, IRR stays at 11.5–15.8%, against 18.8–25.3% in Çanakkale. Çanakkale is one of Türkiye's windiest sites; at the fleet average (33%) and the same price, IRR falls to ~13.8%.
  • A PPA is insurance. In the base case the PPA's equity return is below spot, because the price is discounted by 10%. In return the bank lends more; for Turkish solar, gearing rises from 46–52% to 54–61%. The real difference shows in the bad case: when the spot price falls by 20%, the Çanakkale wind farm's equity IRR drops to 19–34% under spot but only to 25–42% under the PPA.
  • Leverage only helps when the project return exceeds the cost of debt. Turkish solar's after-tax project IRR (9.4–10.8%) is slightly above the interest rate (8.6%); debt lifts the equity return to 10.5–13.5%. For the Çanakkale wind farm the project IRR is 16–21%, so the same debt lifts the equity return to 32–51%. For Iberian solar, however, the project IRR (4.6–6.7%) is below the interest rate (6.9%) in every run, and debt adds nothing.
  • If the tenor outlasts the PPA, risk piles up at the end of the loan. The loan runs 12 years, the PPA 10. The last two years are exposed to the market. Under price stress, the minimum DSCR of PPA-backed Turkish solar falls below 1 (0.93). This is why lenders want the tenor to match the contract term.
  • The cost assumption is not country-specific. Capex is IRENA's regional (European and Iberian solar) or global average (Turkish solar, all wind). A given project's cost may differ; for example, in the single-Europe runs of 8 Nov 2026 a 13% capex increase for the Spanish solar plant lowered IRR by 1.7–2.0 points (12.4–15.3% at $691/kW, 10.7–13.3% at $779/kW). Spain's own country cost could not be verified in this study (the IRENA report could not be accessed); Iberia uses the European average.

6. Limitations

  • Single year: Türkiye's solar capture rate and intraday price shape rest on 2025. The solar capture rate was 91% in 2024 (it fell 8 points in one year); the monthly daily spread varies a lot from year to year (January: 53 in 2024, 37 $/MWh in 2025). The weekend and holiday effect was measured from the 731 days of 2024–2025; in reality the effect of single-day holidays varies widely (midday ratio 0.66–1.10), while the game gives them all the same depth.
  • Unlicensed solar missing: solar in EPİAŞ real-time generation covers licensed plants only. The profile's shape is indicative.
  • Negative prices only in Europe and Iberia: the game matches 2025's share of negative-price hours (6.3–6.6%) and the lowest price; the average depth rests on sample days with many negative hours. In Türkiye the PTF cannot go below zero. In reality some subsidised plants keep generating at negative prices; in the game the player's plant does not generate in those hours. That is why curtailment for the Iberian solar plant is high (23–35%).
  • The rest of Europe follows Germany: all European countries outside Iberia run on Germany's price level and capture rates; data for France, Italy, the Nordics and Britain were not used. Portugal gets the same price as Spain (in reality MIBEL prices split in some hours). Solar's price effect is computed from the cloud cover of a single representative point in each market; Europe's solar capture rate varies by ±4 points between runs.
  • Price path: project economics hold the first game year's price flat for 25 years. The decline in later game years is the game's own behaviour (rival investment, solar saturation), not a forecast for the real market. The real price path depends on fuel prices, exchange rates, demand and installed capacity.
  • Wind targets rest on a single year: the 2025 wind year may differ from the long-term average. Fleet averages include older turbines, while the game's plant is new. The regional weights for Germany and Spain are approximate.
  • Game pacing: the game's own economy (game money) is deliberately compressed. The financial metrics in this note are computed at real-world scale, not in game money.
