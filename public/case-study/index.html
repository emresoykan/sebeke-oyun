Case study • October 2026

Calibrating a game's power price and generation model to market data and linking it to project economics

In Wattlas, players build renewable power plants on a world map. This note explains how the game's hourly electricity price and wind generation models were calibrated to 2025 data, and what the same model implies for LCOE, NPV and IRR at real-world scale. The game interface is in Turkish.

Update (30 Oct 2026): The results were regenerated with the current model. Calibration and project economics now rest on the first game year (120 game days, about one calendar year); the first version used the average of 365 game days (about three calendar years). The European targets now use full-year ENTSO-E 2025 data, and the Turkish solar target was widened from 7 to 12 sample days.

Update (4 Nov 2026): The game now has hydrological years: each year in Türkiye is dry, normal or wet. The results on this page are for a normal year and match the previous model. A new game's first year follows the actual 2026, which was very wet, so the first-year average price in Türkiye is about $51/MWh (about $67 in a normal year). Details: docs/kalibrasyon.md, "Hidrolojik yıl".

Update (8 Nov 2026): Solar capex in the game now depends on the market: in Europe it is IRENA's European average ($779/kW), 1.13 times the global average ($691/kW). The European rows in section 5 were regenerated with 8 new runs; European solar IRR fell from 12.5–15.2% to 10.7–13.3%; the same runs would give 12.4–15.3% at the old cost, so the drop comes from capex alone. The changes in the European wind rows come only from run-to-run variation. The Turkish rows are the 30 Oct 2026 runs: Turkish capex did not change, and 8 new runs with the current model stayed within the same ranges (average price $66.2/MWh, solar IRR 11.8–13.6%, wind IRR 15.7–22.4%).

Update (9 Nov 2026): Türkiye's price model was recalibrated with EPİAŞ full-year hourly data. The Turkish solar capture rate is now measured from all 8,760 hours of 2025 rather than 12 sample days: 0.834 (the earlier ~0.84 estimate is confirmed). The game now has Türkiye's price cap ($100/MWh; in reality 4,500 TL/MWh since April 2026) and an intraday price shape that changes with the season: midday prices fall deeper in spring and autumn, winter is cheaper, summer the most expensive. The annual average price and solar capture rate are unchanged; in a normal year the average daily spread between the highest and lowest price rose from $44.7 to $51.7/MWh (actual 2024–2025: 47–49; 2026: 62). The Turkish rows in section 5 were regenerated with 8 new runs: the solar plant's average realised price fell by ~4% (IRR 10.9–12.7%). Most of the rise in wind IRR comes from a windier weather sample in these runs (capacity factor 41–56%, against 38–47% before). Details: docs/kalibrasyon.md, "Günlük fiyat şekli ve azami fiyat".

Summary

  • Problem: The game's Turkish market pushed midday prices down to 4–9 $/MWh. Because unsold output was curtailed, the solar plant's capacity factor stayed at 8%. Wind generation was also off, in different directions by country: too low in Türkiye, more than twice the actual level in Germany. Financial metrics computed from this model were meaningless.
  • Approach: Targets for the average price and the capture rate were derived from EPİAŞ 2025 data (Türkiye) and ENTSO-E 2025 data (Germany, Spain), and targets for the wind fleet capacity factor from TEİAŞ, EPDK and ENTSO-E. Model parameters were then tuned against these targets using simulations of the first game year (120 game days).
  • Result: In the first game year the average price in Türkiye is 66.6 $/MWh (actual: 66.4). The solar capture rate is 84% (actual: 83.4%) and wind 97% (actual: 98.6%). The wind fleet capacity factor is 32.6% in Türkiye (actual: 32.8%) and 20.2% in Germany (actual: 20.2%). In Europe the average price is 89.5 $/MWh (target: ~88), the solar capture rate 55% (target: ~53%) and wind 88% (target: ~90%).
  • Finding: If first-year prices hold, a solar plant is profitable in both markets at a 10% cost of capital: IRR is 10.9–12.7% in Türkiye and 10.7–13.3% in Europe (where capex is 13% higher). For wind, resource quality decides: the Çanakkale plant earns an IRR of 18.8–25.3%, the northern German one 7.3–13.5%.

1. Why it mattered

For each plant, the game's project economics module takes generation and the average realised price from the simulation. Capex and opex are real-world assumptions. If the simulated price is far from reality, LCOE, NPV and IRR are wrong too: garbage in, garbage out. For the module to mean anything, the price model had to be tested against market data first.

The game's hourly price combines a base daily curve, seasonal demand, evening heating demand, a midday price reduction from solar and a general price reduction from wind. Calibration sets the size of each component. A wind farm's output is computed from the site's hourly wind speed through a turbine power curve; the site's mean speed comes from NASA POWER data.

3. Method

Capture rate: the average price a technology earns in the hours it generates, divided by the flat (baseload) average price. Solar plants all generate at the same hours, so they depress midday prices; the more solar capacity, the lower the ratio. This effect is called cannibalisation.

capture price = Σ (price × generation) ÷ Σ generation capture rate = capture price ÷ average price
  1. Derive targets: compute both metrics from actual data, separately for Türkiye and Europe. Europe is a single market in the game, so the average of Germany and Spain was used as the target (weighted by generation for the wind ratio).
  2. Measure in the game: run the game headless in a browser and advance the first game year: 120 game days, with one game day covering about three calendar days. Rivals invest as in a normal game; event cards are off. Ratios are measured at market (fleet) level: the price is weighted by the market's solar generation profile and the wind fleet's hourly output, including output in hours when the price is zero. That is the figure comparable to actual data.
  3. Tune and repeat: find the cause of each deviation, change the relevant parameter and measure again.

Why it deviated and what changed

  • The market's solar saturation rose by 0.012 per day and hit its cap in about three months. After that, the midday price fell to nearly zero almost every day. The daily increase was cut to 0.002 and the cap was limited to 1.2 times the starting value.
  • The depth of solar's midday price reduction was cut from 57 to 30 $/MWh and wind's effect from 10 to 8. The base price level was raised by 8%.
  • In Europe the price level multiplier was raised from 1.15 to 1.62. The solar effect was set to 3.7 times Türkiye's and the wind effect to 1.7 times. This reflects the data: in both countries solar and wind depress prices more strongly than in Türkiye (lower capture rates).
  • In Europe, wind's price effect is no longer computed from the wind speed at a single representative point but from the hourly output of the German and Spanish wind fleet, weighted by region. Wind blows across a region at once; when the fleet produces a lot, the price falls.
  • In the game, prices fall over the years because rivals build plants every year. The target was therefore defined on the first game year, and Türkiye's price level multiplier was set to 0.92: the first-year average fell from ~72 to ~66 $/MWh.

Wind generation

Capacity factor (CF): a plant's annual output divided by what it would produce running at full power all year. Fleet CF = total generation ÷ (installed capacity × hours).

  • Power curve: a modern turbine like the game's standard one (150 m rotor) reaches rated power at 11 m/s, not 12.
  • Site speed factor: the ~50 km wind data is biased differently by country. In Türkiye wind farms sit on ridges windier than the grid-cell average, so the factor is 1.02; in Germany it is 0.73 and in Spain 0.925. Each factor was chosen so that the game's fleet CF matches actual output. In Türkiye the fleet was weighted by the EPDK provincial distribution.
  • Seasonality: monthly speed multipliers from the 2025 monthly CF of Türkiye, Germany and Spain. Türkiye's windiest month is August (summer northerlies); Germany's is winter.
  • Indicator: the wind dots in the panel are now an expected-CF class. ●●●○○ means 27–34% and includes the Turkish fleet average.

Financing structure: debt, tax and PPA

The same plants were modelled with debt and corporate tax under two sales structures. Under spot, all output is sold to the market. Under a PPA, a fixed price 10% below the expected realised price applies for the first 10 years. The bank sizes the debt so that the minimum DSCR over the tenor meets a target: 1.40 for spot sales, 1.20 under a PPA. The loan has a 12-year tenor with level instalments, and gearing is capped at 75%. In the stress columns the debt is sized on the base case, then the spot price is cut by 20%.

Interpretation

  • Solar earns more than a 10% WACC in both markets. In Türkiye the average realised price ($52–57/MWh) is above LCOE ($45–52/MWh); IRR is 10.9–12.7%. At a 12% WACC, NPV falls on both sides of zero (−$0.25m to +$0.17m): for an unsubsidised, merchant-only solar plant, the cost of financing remains decisive.
  • The result depends on first-year prices holding. In the game, prices fall over the years as rivals invest: in Türkiye the average price is $66/MWh in the first year, $58 in the second and $50 in the third (−25%; normal year, 4 runs, 9 Nov 2026). That is close to the 20% price stress in the financing table. For solar the fall is much faster: as rivals add solar, its capture rate drops from 0.83 to 0.68 and 0.53, and solar's market-wide realised price falls from ~$55 to ~$26/MWh (−52%). The 20% stress does not show this risk for solar. In reality Türkiye's solar capture rate fell from 0.91 to 0.83 between 2024 and 2025; the game's pace (~15 points a year) is faster and is the game's own behaviour, not a forecast. The lower returns in the first version of this page (Turkish solar IRR 9.2–10.2%) came from the average price of 365 game days, i.e. three years including that decline.
  • In Europe part of the higher price is eroded by curtailment. Europe's average price in these runs is ~38% higher than Türkiye's ($91.2 against $66.1/MWh). Prices often fall to zero in solar hours, and the plant curtails 8–13% of its output. Still, the Spanish plant sells at a higher price than the Turkish one ($65–71/MWh). But capex is also 13% higher ($779 against $691/kW): LCOE is $57–63/MWh ($45–52 in Türkiye) and IRR 10.7–13.3%. At a 12% WACC, NPV falls on both sides of zero (−$0.33m to +$0.35m).
  • The European solar result may be optimistic. In the game, the Spanish plant's capture rate, counting curtailed output, is 63–69%, against 55.2% for the actual Spanish fleet and 55% for the game's European fleet. Europe is a single price zone, and solar's price effect is computed from the cloud cover at a representative point in central Germany (50°N 10°E), while the plant generates under its own site's weather. When Spain is sunny and Germany is cloudy, the plant sells at a high price.
  • Weather-year risk. Each run is a different weather year. The northern German wind farm's capacity factor ranged from 20% to 29% across runs, and its NPV fell on both sides of zero (−$1.04m to +$1.47m). This is why projects are judged on a long-term generation estimate rather than a single year; for a merchant project, a PPA or a battery reduces this risk.
  • For wind, resource quality decides. The same turbine runs at a 41–56% capacity factor in Çanakkale and 20–29% in northern Germany. Although the German realised price is ~18% higher, IRR stays at 7.3–13.5%, against 18.8–25.3% in Çanakkale. Çanakkale is one of Türkiye's windiest sites; at the fleet average (33%) and the same price, IRR falls to ~13.8%.
  • A PPA is insurance. In the base case the PPA's equity return is below spot, because the price is discounted by 10%. In return the bank lends more; for Turkish solar, gearing rises from 46–52% to 54–61%. The real difference shows in the bad case: when the spot price falls by 20%, the Çanakkale wind farm's equity IRR drops to 19–34% under spot but only to 25–42% under the PPA.
  • Leverage only helps when the project return exceeds the cost of debt. Turkish solar's after-tax project IRR (9.4–10.8%) is slightly above the interest rate (8.6%); debt lifts the equity return to 10.5–13.5%. For the Çanakkale wind farm the project IRR is 16–21%, so the same debt lifts the equity return to 32–51%. In the northern German wind farm's weakest wind year, the project IRR (6.1%) is below the interest rate (6.9%), and debt adds nothing.
  • If the tenor outlasts the PPA, risk piles up at the end of the loan. The loan runs 12 years, the PPA 10. The last two years are exposed to the market. Under price stress, the minimum DSCR of PPA-backed Turkish solar falls below 1 (0.93). This is why lenders want the tenor to match the contract term.
  • The cost assumption is not country-specific. Capex is IRENA's regional (European solar) or global average (Turkish solar, all wind). A given project's cost may differ; for example, the 13% capex increase for European solar lowered IRR by 1.7–2.0 points in the same runs (12.4–15.3% at $691/kW, 10.7–13.3% at $779/kW).

6. Limitations

  • Single year: Türkiye's solar capture rate and intraday price shape rest on 2025. The solar capture rate was 91% in 2024 (it fell 8 points in one year); the monthly daily spread varies a lot from year to year (January: 53 in 2024, 37 $/MWh in 2025). The model has no weekend or holiday effect.
  • Unlicensed solar missing: solar in EPİAŞ real-time generation covers licensed plants only. The profile's shape is indicative.
  • No negative prices: the game's price floor is 0 and plants curtail at a zero price. Real markets have negative prices, and subsidised plants keep generating. That is why curtailment in the game's European market is high.
  • Single European market: the difference between Germany and Spain is lost in the average. Because solar's price effect is computed from a single representative point's weather, the Spanish solar plant's capture rate comes out above the actual Spanish fleet's (see above). Wind's price effect is computed from the region-weighted fleet; Europe's wind capture rate is close to target (88% against ~90%).
  • Price path: project economics hold the first game year's price flat for 25 years. The decline in later game years is the game's own behaviour (rival investment, solar saturation), not a forecast for the real market. The real price path depends on fuel prices, exchange rates, demand and installed capacity.
  • Wind targets rest on a single year: the 2025 wind year may differ from the long-term average. Fleet averages include older turbines, while the game's plant is new. The regional weights for Germany and Spain are approximate.
  • Game pacing: the game's own economy (game money) is deliberately compressed. The financial metrics in this note are computed at real-world scale, not in game money.

7. Next steps

  • Add the price effect of weekends and holidays to the model.
  • Add negative prices and support schemes to the model.
  • Split Europe into separate country markets.
  • Use pilot player feedback to measure which parts are seen as unrealistic.
